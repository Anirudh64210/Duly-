## 0. The answer in short

1. **Part 2, a cut of the fee, is not allowed in almost every state for a company like Duly.** Rule 5.4(a): "A lawyer or law firm shall not share legal fees with a nonlawyer". Rule 7.2(b): a lawyer shall not give "anything of value to a person for recommending the lawyer's services". Bar ethics opinions applied these rules to Avvo Legal Services, which charged lawyers a "marketing fee" for each service sold. New York found it "an improper payment for a recommendation". New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Ohio found it improper fee sharing (section 6). A per-matter or percentage cut is the same design. In Texas, paying or accepting money "to solicit employment" is barratry, a third-degree felony (Penal Code 38.12). The only clean ways to earn a share of legal fees are (a) becoming a state-certified lawyer referral service, state by state, or (b) owning a law firm through an Arizona ABS, as Boundless does. Both are large changes to what Duly is (section 7).
2. **Part 3, firms paying to stay listed, is allowed only as a flat advertising fee, and only if Duly does not "recommend" the lawyer.** Rule 7.2(b)(1) lets a lawyer "pay the reasonable costs of advertisements". But NC comment [2] defines a recommendation as anything that "endorses or vouches for a lawyer's credentials, abilities, competence, character, or other professional qualities". NC comment [5] bars paying a platform that implies it "has analyzed a person's legal problems when determining which lawyer should receive the referral". **A "curated, vetted" network matched to the student's own case is close to that line.** New York held that Avvo's ratings and its refund guarantee made Avvo a recommender. Counsel must decide where the line falls (section 6.3).
3. **Part 1, discounted access, is legal on its own.** A lawyer may set a lower price for Duly students. The legal question is only how Duly gets paid.
4. **The market is real, and students pay for most of it themselves.** In FY2024, USCIS approved 28,577 NIW petitions and 11,254 EB-1A petitions. In the first three quarters of FY2026 it received 25,722 O petitions, with an RFE rate of 24.2% (USCIS data, section 2). People usually pay for NIW and EB-1A themselves. By regulation, the employer must pay the attorney fees for the H-1B petition itself (20 CFR 655.731(c)(9)). For H-1B, students pay only for advice. No public source says how many F-1, OPT or STEM OPT students hire an attorney. That is the largest gap in this file.
5. **Attorney fees are large compared with Duly's prices.** Published flat fees: consultation $200 to $300; F-1 change of status or reinstatement $1,850; O-1 $4,400 to $5,800; EB-1A $6,800 to $8,500; NIW $6,800; RFE response $650 to $1,450; hourly $400 (section 3). One retained O-1 or NIW client is worth more to a firm than Duly Plus earns in a year from about 115 to 220 paying students at $39 **(estimate)**.
6. **What firms pay today to win a client:** legal search ads average **$131.63 per lead** (LocaliQ 2026). The B2B average cost to win a legal client is **$749** (First Page Sage 2026). LegalMatch membership starts at **$455 a month**. Avvo and Martindale-Nolo publish no prices (section 4, section 5).
7. **A flat listing can earn about as much as a cut would have, without the legal risk.** With 1% to 3% of active students hiring an attorney each year, firms could rationally pay Duly about $75k to $225k a year at 10k students and $0.75M to $2.2M at 100k students. That is the number of clients times the $749 benchmark, charged as a flat fee **(estimate, section 9)**. A 15% cut of the same fees would have earned about the same. At 100k students this would be Duly's largest revenue line, far above Duly Plus ($6k to $15k a year at 10k students, channels.md).
8. **The cleanest second line is software, not referrals.** Firms would pay for Duly's consented case packet to arrive in their intake. That saves them hours of collecting documents. Sell it to any firm the student chooses, at a flat seat price that is not tied to which matters or fees arrive (CA Rule 5.4 comment [2]).
9. **This conflicts with current decisions.** BOUNDARY says "no paid referrals" and allows only a neutral pointer such as the AILA search. VALUE-2 lists referrals under "Still no". Changing that is a founder decision, after counsel (channels.md section 4).

### 2.4 How many students hire a lawyer: no public data

I found no survey or government figure for the share of F-1, OPT or STEM OPT students who pay an immigration attorney in a year. A rough bound **(estimate)**: about 70k self-paid or mixed matters a year nationwide (NIW about 28k, EB-1A about 11k, O about 34k). If a quarter to a half of these involve people who were recently on F-1, OPT or STEM OPT, that is about 18k to 35k matters a year. Compared with a pool of about 450k to 600k people (OPT, STEM OPT and graduate students near graduation), that is roughly **3% to 6% a year for these large matters**, before paid consultations, F-1 problems and RFEs are added. **Plan on 1% to 3% of active Duly students hiring an attorney through Duly each year, and 5% to 15% paying for at least one consultation** **(estimate)**. Replace this with real data: an opt-in question at onboarding ("Have you paid an immigration lawyer in the last 12 months? For what?") and a question to two or three DSOs.

## 6. The rules, quoted

### 6.1 Fee sharing (Rule 5.4)

North Carolina Rule 5.4(a): "A lawyer or law firm shall not share legal fees with a nonlawyer, except that:" ... (6) "a lawyer or law firm may pay a portion of a legal fee to a credit card processor, group advertising provider, or online marketing platform if the amount paid is for payment processing or for administrative or marketing services, and there is no interference with the lawyer's independent professional judgment or with the client-lawyer relationship." NC 5.4(c): "A lawyer shall not permit a person who recommends, engages, or pays the lawyer to render legal services for another to direct or regulate the lawyer's professional judgment" (https://www.ncbar.gov/for-lawyers/ethics-and-governing-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct/51-57-law-firms-and-associations/54-professional-independence-of-a-lawyer/, read 2026-10-01). **Subpart (a)(6) is North Carolina's own addition and is not in the ABA Model Rule (unverified as to the Model text and NC's effective date).** It helps only lawyers licensed in NC.

California Rule 5.4(a), 2018 text: "A lawyer or law firm shall not share legal fees directly or indirectly with a nonlawyer or with an organization that is not authorized to practice law, except that: ... (4) a lawyer or law firm may pay a prescribed registration, referral, or other fee to a lawyer referral service established, sponsored and operated in accordance with the State Bar of California's Minimum Standards for Lawyer Referral Services". Comment [2]: payment to a nonlawyer "for goods and services provided to a lawyer or law firm" is allowed, "however, the compensation to a nonlawyer third-party may not be determined as a percentage or share of the lawyer's or law firm's overall revenues or tied to fees in particular cases or legal matters." (https://www.calbar.ca.gov/Portals/0/documents/rules/Rule_5.4-Exec_Summary-Redline.pdf, read 2026-10-01. The PDF says this version "was superseded by order of the Supreme Court, effective March 22, 2021". **Counsel to confirm the current text.**)

Comment [2] matters for any Duly software sold to firms. Price it per seat or per month. Never price it per matter or as a share of fees.

### 6.2 Paying for recommendations (Rule 7.2(b))

North Carolina Rule 7.2(b): "A lawyer shall not compensate, give, or promise anything of value to a person for recommending the lawyer's services except that a lawyer may (1) pay the reasonable costs of advertisements or communications permitted by this Rule; (2) pay the usual charges of an intermediary organization that complies with Rule 7.4, or a prepaid legal services plan ...; (3) pay for a law practice in accordance with Rule 1.17; and (4) give nominal gifts ..." (https://www.ncbar.gov/for-lawyers/ethics-and-governing-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct/71-76-information-about-legal-services/72-communications-concerning-a-lawyers-services-specific-rules/, read 2026-10-01). The ABA Model Rule 7.2(b)(2) says "a not-for-profit or qualified lawyer referral service" where NC says "intermediary organization" **(unverified: ABA page refused the fetch)**.

- Comment [2]: "A communication contains a recommendation if it endorses or vouches for a lawyer's credentials, abilities, competence, character, or other professional qualities."
- Comment [3]: (b)(1) covers "print directory listings, on-line directory listings, ... sponsorship fees, Internet-based advertisements, and group advertising."
- Comment [5]: "A lawyer may pay others for generating client leads, such as Internet-based client leads, as long as the lead generator does not recommend the lawyer, any payment to the lead generator is consistent with Rules 1.5(e) (division of fees) and 5.4 (professional independence of the lawyer) ... a lawyer must not pay a lead generator that states, implies, or creates a reasonable impression that it is recommending the lawyer, is making the referral without payment from the lawyer, or has analyzed a person's legal problems when determining which lawyer should receive the referral."

### 6.3 The Avvo opinions: the closest precedent

NYSBA Ethics Opinion 1132 (August 8, 2017), https://nysba.org/ethics-opinion-1132/ (read 2026-10-01):
- The facts: a "$149 document review results in the lawyer receiving the full $149 client payment, but Avvo separately charges a $40 marketing fee".
- The holding: "A lawyer paying Avvo's current marketing fee for Avvo Legal Services is making an improper payment for a recommendation in violation of Rule 7.2(a)."
- Why: Avvo's ratings gave the impression that a "10" lawyer "is 'superb,' and is thus a better lawyer" (¶31). Avvo "effectively 'vouches for' each participating lawyer's credentials, abilities, and competence by offering a full refund if the client is not satisfied" (¶35).
- It left fee sharing open (¶15). It cites New Jersey 732 (2017: lawyers "may not participate in Avvo"), Pennsylvania 2016-200 ("impermissible fee sharing") and Ohio 2016-3 ("unethical"). I could not fetch those three opinions **(unverified beyond NY's description)**.

**What this means for Duly's design.** Three things the founder wants each move Duly toward "recommendation": ratings or badges such as "top", "best" or "vetted"; matching a lawyer to the student's case; and a Duly-backed refund. Once Duly recommends, a lawyer may pay Duly only as a qualified or certified referral service.

ABA Formal Opinion 465 (2013) on "deal of the day" coupons. As I remember it, it allowed lawyers to use coupon sites that keep part of the price, as an advertising cost, with conditions on refunds, conflicts and trust accounting. **(unverified: the ABA PDF refused the fetch.)** Counsel should read it, because a "discounted Duly price" sold through Duly is close to that model.

### 6.4 Lawyer referral services: the licensed path to a cut, in California

- Bus. & Prof. Code 6155(a): no entity "shall ... operate for the direct or indirect purpose, in whole or in part, of referring potential clients to attorneys" unless "certified by the State Bar of California", and "The combined charges to the potential client by the referral service and the attorney ... do not exceed the total cost that the client would normally pay if no referral service were involved." (https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=6155, read 2026-10-01.) **This may reach a Duly that "matches" California students to attorneys even if no attorney pays Duly. Counsel should check the 6155 exceptions (unverified).**
- State Bar Rules 3.800 to 3.829 (https://www.calbar.ca.gov/sites/default/files/portals/0/documents/rules/Rules_Title3_Div5-Ch3-LRS.pdf, read 2026-10-01):
  - 3.822(A): a governing committee of three or more, a majority of them active California licensees.
  - 3.823(B): "At least twenty attorney members, ten of whom are from separate and independent law firms". 3.823(C)(1): panel members carry errors and omissions insurance.
  - 3.824: "Only an active licensee of the State Bar practicing in the community served". **Out-of-state immigration lawyers cannot be on the panel**, even though they may practise federal immigration law.
  - 3.825(B): "Panel membership fees may not in any way be based on or guarantee contacts, calls, cases, referrals, or clients." Fees per referral or per fee come through 5.4(a)(4) instead. The exact fees a certified service may charge are in the Minimum Standards and Schedule of Charges, which I did not read **(unverified)**.
  - 3.826(A): referrals must be "allocated equitably to panel members", so the service cannot send every case to its favourite firm. 3.826(C) sets duties for automated referrals: follow up within 2 business days for the first 12 months, and "forgo any fee if a referral is made that does not accurately reflect the needs of the client".
  - 3.823(D): it "may disclose a panel member's past performance when the information is accurate, complete, and not misleading."
- Other states run their own systems. Texas, Florida and others certify or regulate referral services **(unverified; counsel to map)**.

North Carolina Rule 7.4 (intermediary organizations, https://www.ncbar.gov/for-lawyers/ethics-and-governing-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct/71-76-information-about-legal-services/74-intermediary-organizations/, read 2026-10-01) covers a "lawyer referral service, lawyer advertising cooperative, lawyer matching service, online marketing platform". It requires the platform to make "the criteria for inclusion available to prospective clients, including any payment made or arranged by the lawyer(s)". It also requires that the platform "does not require the lawyer to pay more than a reasonable sum representing a proportional share of the organization's administrative and advertising costs."

### 6.5 Owning the law firm: Arizona ABS and Utah

- Arizona: "An alternative business structure, or ABS, is a business entity that includes nonlawyers who have an economic interest or decision-making authority in a firm and provides legal services in accord with Supreme Court Rules 31 and 31.1(c)." It is governed by ACJA § 7-209. The directory lists 181 entities, including Boundless Legal, LLC (Active) and several immigration firms (iVisa Legal, Crimson Talent Immigration, AILS Law Group, Motion Law, and others). Source: https://www.azcourts.gov/cld/Alternative-Business-Structure and /Directory (read 2026-10-01).
- Federal immigration practice is open to an attorney admitted in any state (8 CFR 1.1/292.1, **unverified: eCFR refused the fetch**). So an Arizona ABS with attorneys licensed anywhere can serve students nationally. That is Boundless's structure. The attorneys' own states may still restrict working for a firm with nonlawyer owners (choice of law, Rule 8.5; ABA Formal Op. 499 on passive investment, **unverified**).
- Utah: "The Sandbox is closed to new applications". "The Court closed the alternative business structure (ABS)-only portion of the Sandbox at the end of 2024". It is "set to sunset on August 14, 2027" (https://utahinnovationoffice.org/, read 2026-10-01). Utah is not a path.

### 6.6 Other rules that apply

- **Texas Penal Code 38.12, barratry**: an offense "with intent to obtain economic benefit" includes "(4) pays or gives or offers to pay or give a person money or anything of value to solicit employment" and "(6) accepts or agrees to accept money or anything of value to solicit employment". A lawyer who "knowingly accepts employment ... that results from the solicitation" also commits an offense. It is "a felony of the third degree." (https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._penal_code_section_38.12, read 2026-10-01.) How it applies to advertising platforms and its exceptions is **(unverified)**. It is a reason to keep every payment flat and to stay out of per-client deals.
- **8 CFR 1003.102** (discipline of immigration practitioners): (d) solicitation "through the use of runners"; (f) "a false or misleading communication about his or her qualifications or services" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/8/1003.102, read 2026-10-01). Duly's copy about listed lawyers must be true and specific.
- **State immigration-consultant laws.** California allows consultants to make "referrals to persons who could undertake legal representation activities". Illinois allows only fee-free referrals (upl_liability.md, citing Cal. B&P 22441 and 815 ILCS 505/2AA). A student-paid "matching fee" could make Duly look like a paid immigration consultant. Do not charge students for the referral itself.
- **BOUNDARY and the words Duly uses.** Duly never calls itself a lawyer or an attorney, and the step-back card keeps a neutral AILA pointer (channels.md section 4).

Leave D and E for a later decision with counsel. C is not available.

## 8. How a curated network could stand out, and where curation runs into Rule 7.2

What students lack today, from the sources above:
- **Prices are hidden.** Most O-1, EB-1A and NIW firms require a consultation before quoting (Manifest, WeGreened, Alma). Marketplaces show consult prices but not case prices (Lawfully).
- **No one checks the lawyers.** Lawfully "does not verify or warrant the accuracy of information included on the profiles". It does not say how lawyers are ranked or whether they pay for placement.
- **Intake is slow and repeated.** Every new firm asks for the I-20s, EADs, I-94 and employment history again. Duly already keeps these.
- **Response time.** Firm unresponsiveness is a common consumer complaint. Clio's "secret shopper" studies reported that many firms never answered **(unverified: clio.com refused the fetch)**.

Ways to stand out that stay **objective** and do not "vouch" **(design proposals for counsel)**:

| Feature | Why it helps | Rule-safe wording |
|---|---|---|
| Published fixed prices for common student matters (30-min consult, F-1 reinstatement, OPT RFE, O-1, NIW, EB-1A), and a Duly-student price set by each firm | Removes the hidden-price problem; the "discount" the founder wants | "Prices set by each firm. Duly does not set or receive any part of legal fees." |
| A response-time commitment the firm signs (for example a reply within 1 business day), with the measured median shown | Speed is what a worried student needs most | Show facts ("median first reply: 6 business hours, last 90 days"), not grades. Facts must be "accurate, complete, and not misleading" (CA 3.823(D)) |
| Objective admission criteria shown to every student: licence in good standing (checked against the state bar), share of practice in immigration, malpractice insurance, number of F-1, OPT or O-1 matters handled | Quality without a rating | "Listed firms meet these published criteria. Duly does not rank or recommend any firm." (NC 7.4 requires showing the criteria and "any payment made ... by the lawyer(s)") |
| Neutral ordering: random, or filtered by the student by language, state or matter type | Avoids "Duly analyzed your problem and picked a lawyer" (NC 7.2 comment [5]) | "Order is random each time you open this list." |
| A consented packet handoff (model G) | Cuts intake time for both sides; uses Duly's strongest feature | The student chooses the firm and what to share; nothing is shared without consent |
| No Duly-backed refund or outcome guarantee | NY 1132 ¶35 treated Avvo's refund as vouching | Firms may offer their own terms, shown as theirs |
| Disclosure on every listing | 7.2 comment [5] bars implying the listing is unpaid | "Firms pay Duly a flat listing fee. The fee is the same for every firm and does not depend on whether you hire them." |

**The tension, plainly.** The more Duly "curates", the more it "recommends". The more it recommends, the fewer payment models are legal. "Vetted" and "top attorneys" are the words to avoid. "Meets published criteria" is the safer form.

## 9. What it could earn (estimates)

The assumptions are all **(estimate)** unless a source is given.
- Active students on Duly: 10k or 100k.
- Share who hire an attorney through Duly in a year: 1% to 3% (section 2.4).
- Average fee per retained matter: about $4,000 (a mix of $1,850 F-1 work and $5,800 to $8,500 O-1 and green card work, section 3).
- What a rational firm will pay to win a client: about $749 (First Page Sage blended CAC). Firms stop renewing a listing that costs more than the clients it brings.

| | 10k active students | 100k active students |
|---|---|---|
| Retained matters through Duly a year (1% to 3%) | 100 to 300 | 1,000 to 3,000 |
| Legal fees those matters generate | $0.4M to $1.2M | $4M to $12M |
| **Model A/B ceiling: flat listings worth what the clients cost to win elsewhere** (matters × $749) | **$75k to $225k a year** | **$0.75M to $2.2M a year** |
| As listings, for example | 25 firms at $250 to $750 a month | 60 to 120 firms at $1,000 to $1,500 a month |
| For comparison, model C (not allowed): 15% of fees | $60k to $180k | $0.6M to $1.8M |
| For comparison, Duly Plus | about $6k to $15k a year (channels.md) | about $60k to $150k (scaled, estimate) |

What follows from the table:
- **At scale, the attorney network would be the largest revenue line, as the founder believes.** It earns this legally only as flat listings priced to the client volume firms actually get, which is how LegalMatch prices by "location(s) and legal specialization(s)".
- **Below about 10k active students, firms will not pay much.** Each firm would get only a few clients a year. Start with a few firms at a low founding price, or free, until there is data on clients per firm. That data sets the price.
- Model G (intake software) adds revenue that does not depend on referrals: for example 50 to 200 firm seats at $50 to $150 a month, which is $30k to $360k a year **(estimate; test the price with firms)**.

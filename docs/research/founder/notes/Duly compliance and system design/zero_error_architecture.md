# Zero-Error Recommendation/Timeline Architecture for a Consumer Immigration App (Duly): Rules-as-Code + LLM Narration, Rule Ingestion, and QA Regime (2024-2026)

## Key Question 1: Rules-as-code precedents and their structures (OpenFisca, Catala, PolicyEngine) and lessons for immigration rules

### Takeaway
Mature rules-as-code systems (OpenFisca, Catala, PolicyEngine) all converge on the same core pattern: legal rules encoded as versioned, effective-dated parameters in declarative files, with per-value legal citations as provenance, and reforms applied by adding new dated values rather than mutating old ones. This maps directly onto immigration clocks and windows (90/150-day unemployment clocks, OPT filing windows, the 2026 fixed-term admission rule, grace periods): each threshold becomes a dated parameter with a citation, and "what was the rule on date X" becomes a lookup, not a code change.

### Cited Findings
- OpenFisca encodes legal parameters in hierarchical YAML files where each parameter holds `values` keyed by effective dates; the docs illustrate that a parameter can be "undefined before 1993; equal to 1000 from 1993 to 2010; equal to 1500 in 2010" — so time-travel queries are native, and updates are new dated entries, never overwrites — [OpenFisca legislation parameters docs](https://openfisca.org/doc/coding-the-legislation/legislation_parameters.html)
- OpenFisca parameters support per-value metadata including a **reference** field (URL/citation to the legal text) and a **unit** field; values can be flagged `expected` to represent announced-but-not-yet-effective changes (directly applicable to the D/S rule's 60-day delayed effective date) — [OpenFisca legislation parameters docs](https://openfisca.org/doc/coding-the-legislation/legislation_parameters.html)
- OpenFisca markets itself on "legible parameters and executable code" providing algorithmic transparency, with open-source country packages maintained collaboratively — [OpenFisca.org](https://openfisca.org/en/)
- Catala (Merigoux et al., Inria) is a programming language for statutory law built on the argument that "prose remains a woefully inadequate tool" for legal algorithms; it pairs lawyers with programmers over a shared literate-programming medium, uses default logic to mirror statutes' general-rule-plus-exceptions structure, and its core compilation is formally verified in the F* proof assistant — [Catala: A Programming Language for the Law, arXiv:2103.03198](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03198)
- Catala was validated on real legislation (US Internal Revenue Code Section 121 and French family benefits), producing "correct-by-construction executable specifications," and the formalization process itself uncovered a bug in an official government implementation — [Catala paper, arXiv:2103.03198](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03198)
- Catala is being used with French administrations (DGFiP tax authority, CNAF family benefits) as a route to "more reliable administration" of law — [Inria: CATALA translates law into code](https://www.inria.fr/en/catala-software-dgfip-cnaf)
- PolicyEngine maintains open-source US/UK tax-benefit rules engines (built on the OpenFisca architecture) and publishes a "canonical versioned model paper... description and validation" for each country model — i.e., the validation document is itself versioned alongside the rules — [PolicyEngine US paper repo](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-us-paper); [policyengine-us](https://github.com/policyengine/policyengine-us); [JOSS paper](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/34b9f72df98e586d7e6a2448922407e7)
- PolicyEngine validates its engine by emulating NBER's TAXSIM and comparing outputs case-by-case (differential testing against an independent reference implementation) — [PolicyEngine TAXSIM](https://www.policyengine.org/us/taxsim); [policyengine-taxsim repo](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-taxsim)
- The concrete immigration payload this must encode: DHS's final rule (published July 17, 2026, effective ~September 15, 2026) replaces duration of status with fixed admission — F-1s admitted up to 4 years plus a grace period cut from 60 to 30 days; extensions of stay must be filed with USCIS; untimely filers "begin accruing unlawful presence immediately"; timely-filed F-1 extensions get automatic 240-day employment-authorization extensions; transition rules exempt existing F-1s with pending OPT applications — [Fragomen alert on the DHS final rule](https://www.fragomen.com/insights/united-states-dhs-issues-final-rule-replacing-admission-for-duration-of-status-with-fixed-periods-of-stay-for-f-j-and-i-nonimmigrants.html); [Federal Register final rule 2026-14439](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/17/2026-14439/establishing-a-fixed-time-period-of-admission-and-an-extension-of-stay-procedure-for-nonimmigrant); [Duane Morris alert (effective Sept 15, 2026)](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/dhs_eliminates_duration_status_f_j_i_nonimmigrants_fixed_admission_periods_new_extension_0726.html)

### Inferences
- Duly's rule pack should be an OpenFisca-style store: one YAML/JSON parameter per legal quantity (e.g. `f1.grace_period_days`: 60 until 2026-09-14 with 8 CFR citation, 30 from 2026-09-15 citing 91 FR 2026-14439; `opt.unemployment_limit_days`: 90; `stem_opt.unemployment_limit_days`: 150; `opt.filing_window`: [-90d before program end, +60d after]), each dated value carrying `reference` (citation + URL) and `source_snapshot_hash`. Cohort-dependent transition rules (pending-OPT exemptions) argue for Catala-style explicit scope conditions, not just scalar parameters.
- The 2026 D/S rule is the perfect stress test for the architecture: same student, different answers before/after Sept 15, 2026, plus cohort carve-outs. A system that stores rules as dated values answers this correctly by construction; a prompt-embedded-rules system cannot.
- PolicyEngine's practice suggests Duly should maintain (a) a versioned validation doc per rule-pack release and (b) where possible a differential oracle (e.g., cross-check timeline outputs against school DSO guidance pages or a second independent implementation of the date math).

### Gaps
- No published example found of a production **immigration**-specific rules-as-code engine (OpenFisca/Catala deployments are tax/benefits); Duly would be applying the pattern to a new domain, and immigration-specific pitfalls (SEVIS data dependencies, discretionary adjudication) are not covered in these precedents.

## Key Question 2: The determinism boundary — measured LLM failure rates on legal/date reasoning, and code-computes/LLM-narrates patterns

### Takeaway
Even purpose-built legal RAG products hallucinate 17-33% of the time (Stanford RegLab, preregistered study), and dedicated temporal-reasoning benchmarks exist precisely because LLMs are unreliable at date/interval arithmetic. The defensible architecture is: a deterministic engine computes every date, deadline, and eligibility verdict; the LLM receives the engine's structured output and may only rephrase it (schema-constrained), with abstention when inputs are outside the rule pack's coverage.

### Cited Findings
- Stanford RegLab/HAI's "Hallucination-Free?" study — "the first preregistered empirical evaluation of AI-driven legal research tools" — found Lexis+ AI, Westlaw AI-Assisted Research, and Ask Practical Law AI hallucinate at rates between 17% and 33%, better than GPT-4 alone but far from vendors' hallucination-free claims: "Providers' claims are overstated" — [Stanford RegLab publication page](https://reglab.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free-assessing-the-reliability-of-leading-ai-legal-research-tools/)
- The same study found "Substantial differences between systems in responsiveness and accuracy," i.e., even among professional legal tools quality varies widely — [Stanford RegLab](https://reglab.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free-assessing-the-reliability-of-leading-ai-legal-research-tools/)
- Google's "Test of Time" benchmark (ICLR/NeurIPS-track, 2024) was created because prior temporal evaluations "rely on real-world data that LLMs may have encountered during pre-training"; it uses synthetic date/interval problems and shows LLM performance varies with problem structure, size, question type, and fact order — i.e., date arithmetic reliability is input-shape-dependent, not stable — [Test of Time, arXiv:2406.09170](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09170); [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=44CoQe6VCq)
- Follow-on 2024-2026 temporal benchmarks (TimeBench 2024; EvolveBench ACL 2025 on outdated-knowledge handling; neuro-symbolic temporal reasoning at ACL Findings 2025) confirm temporal reasoning remains an open weakness actively studied, with neuro-symbolic (code-assisted) approaches proposed as the fix — [TIMEBENCH (ACL 2024)](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.66.pdf); [EvolveBench (ACL 2025)](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.788.pdf); [Neuro-Symbolic Temporal Reasoning (ACL Findings 2025)](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.972.pdf)
- Anthropic now offers structured outputs guaranteeing schema-conformant JSON from Claude, which multiple 2025-2026 engineering writeups describe as schema-guaranteed response generation — the mechanism for forcing the narration layer to emit only fields the deterministic engine supplied — [Claude Platform docs: Structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs); [Tessl: Anthropic brings structured outputs to Claude](https://tessl.io/blog/anthropic-brings-structured-outputs-to-claude-developer-platform-making-api-responses-more-reliable)

### Inferences
- Determinism boundary for Duly: the rules engine owns (1) all date arithmetic (grace-period ends, unemployment-day counters, filing windows, I-94 expiry), (2) all eligibility verdicts, (3) all rule-version selection by effective date. The LLM owns only: converting the engine's JSON verdict into plain language, answering "what does this mean" questions grounded in retrieved rule text, and intake parsing (mapping user documents/answers into engine inputs — which must then be confirmed by the user, since intake is also an error source).
- Enforce the boundary mechanically, not by prompt discipline: the narration prompt contains the engine's computed dates as the only dates; a post-generation validator regex-extracts every date/number in the LLM output and rejects the response if any value is absent from the engine's output set (a cheap, deterministic "no new facts" check). Structured outputs make this tractable.
- Abstention design: when a user's situation references a parameter or scenario absent from the rule pack (e.g., a visa category not yet encoded, or a date range where an injunction creates uncertainty), the engine returns `status: OUT_OF_SCOPE` and the product shows a canned "we can't compute this, talk to your DSO/attorney" card — the LLM is never asked to fill the gap. The RegLab 17-33% figure is the argument for why.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve exact per-model accuracy percentages from the Test of Time paper (abstract only; OpenReview blocked by bot check) — the report writer should cite it qualitatively or pull the PDF for numbers.
- No 2025-2026 benchmark found that measures LLM accuracy specifically on **immigration** date computations (OPT windows, unemployment clocks); LegalBench covers legal reasoning tasks broadly but I found no immigration-date subset with published failure rates.

## Key Question 3: Keeping rules current — authoritative feeds, APIs, volumes, and content-ops sizing

### Takeaway
The authoritative chain is machine-readable end to end: Federal Register API (no auth) for rules with effective dates, USCIS Policy Manual updates page + newsroom alerts for sub-regulatory policy, SEVP/Study in the States for F-1 operational guidance, and CourtListener docket-alert webhooks for injunctions that suspend rules. Relevant F-1/OPT items are low-volume (a few per week, not per day), which is what makes a one-founder + part-time-attorney editorial pipeline feasible — every ingested item goes through attorney sign-off before the rule pack changes.

### Cited Findings
- The Federal Register "provides multiple public API endpoints" with no authentication, covering rules, proposed rules, notices and presidential documents, with agency filtering and links to official PDF/XML renditions — the primary machine-readable source for DHS/USCIS/ICE rulemaking and effective dates — [Federal Register API v1 documentation](https://www.federalregister.gov/developers/documentation/api/v1)
- USCIS maintains a dedicated Policy Manual "Updates" page listing each policy alert as a dated PDF (e.g., an Aug 5, 2026 evidentiary-standards alert; a Dec 4, 2025 employment-authorization-validity alert), plus a separate newsroom Alerts page — both are scrape/monitor targets (the updates page returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch, implying scraping needs browser-grade requests or the GovDelivery email subscription instead) — [USCIS Policy Manual Updates](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/updates); [USCIS Alerts](https://www.uscis.gov/newsroom/alerts); [example policy alert PDF](https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/policy-manual-updates/20251204-EmploymentAuthorizationValidity.pdf)
- Policy Manual changes can be consequential and sudden: an August 2026 update expanded USCIS authority to deny incomplete filings without first issuing RFEs/NOIDs — the kind of sub-regulatory change (no Federal Register rule) an FR-only pipeline would miss — [Jackson Lewis Global Immigration Blog](https://www.globalimmigrationblog.com/2026/08/uscis-updates-policy-manual-expanding-authority-to-deny-incomplete-filings-without-first-issuing-rfes-or-noids/)
- CourtListener (Free Law Project) offers five webhook event types including docket alerts (new filings on subscribed cases) and search alerts (new results for saved queries), pushed immediately via POST with up to seven retries over ~54 hours; webhooks are among "advanced features" needing a fee-based agreement with the nonprofit — [CourtListener webhooks docs](https://www.courtlistener.com/help/api/webhooks/) ([canonical wiki page](https://wiki.free.law/c/courtlistener/help/api/webhooks/about)); [Alert APIs](https://wiki.free.law/c/courtlistener/help/api/rest/v4/alerts)
- Law-firm alert practice shows the editorial model: Fragomen, Duane Morris, and Ogletree each published client alerts on the D/S final rule within days of the July 17, 2026 publication, summarizing effective date, transition rules, and action items — event-driven cadence, not daily digests — [Fragomen](https://www.fragomen.com/insights/united-states-dhs-issues-final-rule-replacing-admission-for-duration-of-status-with-fixed-periods-of-stay-for-f-j-and-i-nonimmigrants.html); [Duane Morris](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/dhs_eliminates_duration_status_f_j_i_nonimmigrants_fixed_admission_periods_new_extension_0726.html); [Ogletree](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/dhs-publishes-final-rule-replacing-duration-of-status-with-fixed-admission-periods-for-f-j-and-i-nonimmigrants/)
- University ISS offices (Wisconsin, Columbia, NYU, OSU, UCLA) publish plain-language FAQ pages tracking the same rule changes — a free secondary-source corpus for cross-checking Duly's interpretations against how DSOs read the rule — [UW-Madison ISS summary](https://iss.wisc.edu/summary-of-the-proposed-duration-of-status-rule-change/); [Columbia ISSO](https://isso.columbia.edu/fixedtimeperiod); [NYU OGS FAQ](https://www.nyu.edu/about/leadership-university-administration/organization-directory/office-of-global-services/immigration-news-and-updates/changes-to-duration-of-status-FAQ.html)

### Inferences
- Pipeline design: (1) daily Federal Register API poll filtered to DHS/USCIS/ICE/State + F-1/SEVP keywords; (2) change-detection on the USCIS Policy Manual updates page, uscis.gov alerts, and Study in the States/SEVP broadcast pages (email subscriptions as the robust fallback given the 403-to-bots posture); (3) CourtListener search + docket alerts on litigation challenging any rule in the pack (the D/S rule is near-certain to be litigated; an injunction must be able to flip a rule-pack value back within hours). Every hit lands in a review queue; nothing auto-activates.
- Volume/staffing: FR publishes hundreds of documents daily government-wide, but after agency+topic filtering the F-1/OPT-relevant stream observed across these sources is on the order of a few items per week with rare spikes (a major rule like D/S generates one intense week). A realistic ops load is ~2-5 attorney-review hours/week steady-state, surging around major rules — compatible with one founder doing triage/encoding and a part-time attorney approving diffs. Law-firm alert timing (days, not hours) sets the market bar Duly must meet or beat.
- Each rule-pack change should be a git-style diff (parameter, old value, new value, effective date, citation, source snapshot) that the attorney approves as a unit — mirroring how PolicyEngine ships versioned model releases.

### Gaps
- No published figures found for Fragomen/Envoy/NAFSA internal editorial staffing or exact alert cadence (proprietary); the staffing estimate above is an inference from observed alert frequency, not a cited number.
- USCIS pages block simple automated fetches (403); did not verify whether USCIS offers a structured RSS/API for policy alerts beyond GovDelivery email — needs a build-time check.

## Key Question 4: QA regime — golden tests, property-based testing, LLM evals, citation verification, staged rollout, audit logs

### Takeaway
The credible "cannot break" claim is a stack: golden-case regression suites and property-based tests on the deterministic engine; differential testing against an independent oracle (the PolicyEngine/TAXSIM pattern); CI-gated LLM evals (promptfoo-style) on the narration layer with citation checks; canary rollout of rule-pack versions; and an append-only audit log recording rule-pack version, sources, model, and prompt for every answer.

### Cited Findings
- Promptfoo is the established open-source pattern for treating "LLM prompts like code: regression evals in CI/CD" — declarative test cases with assertions (contains/equals/LLM-rubric) run on every change, blocking merges on regressions — [promptfoo CI/CD regression evals writeup](https://medium.com/@alexrodriguesj/testing-llm-prompts-like-code-regression-evals-in-ci-cd-with-promptfoo-5242b4dcb9be); [promptfoo unit-testing guide](https://www.mager.co/blog/2026-02-23-promptfoo-llm-validation/)
- Braintrust positions itself as the hosted alternative (datasets, scorers, experiment diffs, production logging feeding back into evals); 2026 comparisons frame promptfoo as the CI-native open-source choice and Braintrust as the managed eval/observability platform — [Braintrust vs. Promptfoo comparison](https://www.braintrust.dev/articles/braintrust-vs-promptfoo); [2026 eval-tool roundup](https://www.braintrust.dev/articles/best-prompt-evaluation-tools-2025)
- PolicyEngine's TAXSIM emulator is a working example of differential testing for a rules engine: same inputs run through PolicyEngine and NBER TAXSIM, discrepancies triaged — [policyengine-taxsim](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-taxsim)
- Catala demonstrates the strongest form of engine assurance — formal verification (F* proofs of the compiler) plus the empirical result that formalization "uncovered a bug in an official implementation" — [Catala paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03198)
- Anthropic structured outputs give schema-level guarantees on the narration layer's shape, removing JSON-parse failures as a class and making field-level validators (dates must match engine output) reliable to apply — [Claude structured outputs docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)
- The Stanford RegLab study is itself the template for the eval harness: preregistered question set, hallucination defined as unfaithful-to-source citation, measured per-system — Duly can replicate this internally with an immigration question bank scored for groundedness against the rule pack — [Stanford RegLab](https://reglab.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free-assessing-the-reliability-of-leading-ai-legal-research-tools/)

### Inferences
- Engine QA: (1) golden suite — every historical and hypothetical case (each D/S transition cohort, each OPT window edge: filed day 89 vs 91, leap years, DST-agnostic UTC dates) pinned as input→expected-output fixtures, grown every time a bug or attorney correction occurs; (2) property-based tests (Hypothesis-style) over generated student timelines asserting invariants: grace period end > program end; unemployment days never negative or > limit+1; rule-version selection is monotone in effective dates; recomputing under rule-pack vN-1 reproduces archived answers byte-for-byte; (3) differential oracle where available (e.g., re-implement date math twice — engine + independent script — and diff in CI).
- Narration QA: promptfoo suite run in CI on every prompt/model/rule-pack change, asserting (a) all dates/numbers in output ⊆ engine output, (b) every legal claim carries a citation ID that resolves to retrieved rule text (citation-verification step), (c) refusal behavior on out-of-scope probes. Optionally a second-model verifier scoring groundedness before high-stakes pushes.
- Rollout: rule-pack versions ship like code — staging environment replays the entire golden suite plus a sample of real (anonymized) user timelines under the new pack, diffs any changed answer for attorney review, then canary to a small user slice with monitoring before full activation. Rollback = repointing to the prior pack version (immutable packs make this instant).
- Audit log as legal defense: append-only record per answer: {timestamp, user-case snapshot hash, rule-pack version, parameter values used with citations, engine output, retrieval set, model + version, full prompt, final rendered text, reviewer approvals for the pack}. This makes any disputed answer fully reconstructible — the property TurboTax relies on to run its accuracy guarantee (Q5).

### Gaps
- No public case study found of a legal-tech company publishing its full internal eval/QA regime for a consumer legal product (vendors keep this proprietary); the regime above is assembled from adjacent published practice (PolicyEngine, RegLab methodology, promptfoo/Braintrust patterns), not a single documented precedent.

## Key Question 5: Incident library — real-world consequences defining the failure bar

### Takeaway
The precedents establish that (a) companies are legally liable for what their AI tells consumers (Moffatt v. Air Canada), (b) fabricated legal output draws court sanctions and professional discipline (Mata v. Avianca and successors), and (c) the consumer-software liability norm is a money-backed accuracy guarantee (TurboTax). For immigration specifically the stakes are one-way: the 2026 rule makes a missed extension deadline trigger immediate unlawful-presence accrual, so a single wrong date can cost a user their status.

### Cited Findings
- Moffatt v. Air Canada (BC Civil Resolution Tribunal, Feb 2024): Air Canada was held liable for negligent misrepresentation after its website chatbot gave a passenger wrong bereavement-fare information; the tribunal rejected the argument that the chatbot was a separate entity — companies "remain liable for information provided by AI chatbot" — [ABA Business Law Today](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/); [McCarthy Tétrault case comment](https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/moffatt-v-air-canada-misrepresentation-ai-chatbot); [CanLII commentary](https://www.canlii.org/en/commentary/doc/2025CanLIIDocs1963)
- Mata v. Avianca (S.D.N.Y. 2023): lawyers sanctioned ($5,000) for filing ChatGPT-fabricated case citations — the founding incident of the fake-citation sanctions line — [Wikipedia: Mata v. Avianca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mata_v._Avianca,_Inc.); [Fortune coverage](https://fortune.com/2023/06/23/lawyers-fined-filing-chatgpt-hallucinations-in-court/)
- The pattern continued into 2024+: a B.C. lawyer was reprimanded and ordered to pay costs for inserting ChatGPT-invented cases into court documents — [CBC News](https://cbc.ca/amp/1.7126393)
- TurboTax's consumer liability norm: Intuit's 100% Accurate Calculation Guarantee pays IRS/state penalties and interest caused by a TurboTax calculation error, with a formal claim process — the software owns calculation errors financially — [TurboTax guarantees](https://turbotax.intuit.com/corp/guarantees/); [claim process](https://ttlc.intuit.com/turbotax-support/en-us/help-article/intuit-account-billing/submit-claim-turbotax-100-accurate-calculation-100/L88SYklBL_US_en_US)
- The immigration-specific stakes: under the 2026 final rule, nonimmigrants without timely-filed extensions "begin accruing unlawful presence immediately" after their fixed admission period ends — unlawful presence triggers 3/10-year reentry bars, so a wrong deadline is not recoverable the way a tax penalty is — [Fragomen alert](https://www.fragomen.com/insights/united-states-dhs-issues-final-rule-replacing-admission-for-duration-of-status-with-fixed-periods-of-stay-for-f-j-and-i-nonimmigrants.html)
- Legal-industry deadline-management vendors market explicitly on the "missed deadline" failure mode as the top malpractice driver in immigration practice — [eImmigration blog on missed deadlines](https://get.eimmigration.com/blog/best-software-immigration-law-firms-missed-deadlines-trust-account-mistakes)

### Inferences
- Failure bar for Duly: any date shown to a user is a representation Duly can be held to (Air Canada logic); the defense posture is (1) deterministic computation with full audit trail, (2) conservative framing (earliest-safe-deadline display with buffer), (3) explicit UPL-avoiding disclaimers that do not, per Moffatt, actually eliminate liability for wrong facts — so correctness, not disclaimers, is the real control.
- A TurboTax-style guarantee ("if our computed deadline was wrong under the rules in force, we pay X") is only rational once the deterministic engine + audit log exist; it could then be a differentiating trust signal, but the irreversibility of status loss means no guarantee makes users whole — another argument for the human-review gates in Q6.

### Gaps
- No documented incident found of a consumer **immigration-tech** product harming users through a wrong computed date/rule (searches surfaced only marketing content and mandamus-delay litigation); either such incidents are unreported/settled or the category is too young. The failure bar therefore has to be constructed from adjacent domains (airline chatbot, court sanctions, tax software).

## Key Question 6: Human-in-loop gating — what requires pre-publication approval vs runtime-only checks

### Takeaway
Split by blast radius and reversibility: anything that changes answers for many users at once (rule-pack activation, "this news affects your case" pushes, new visa-category coverage) gets pre-publication attorney approval; per-user runtime outputs ride on the already-approved pack plus automated validators, with escalation-to-human only on low-confidence/out-of-scope flags.

### Cited Findings
- Catala's core workflow is itself a human-in-the-loop design: lawyers and programmers pair on the formalization so legal experts validate the encoding before it executes — the precedent for attorney-approved rule diffs — [Catala paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03198)
- OpenFisca's `expected` value flag lets announced future changes be encoded and reviewed ahead of their effective date, then activate automatically on the date — pre-approval decoupled from activation timing — [OpenFisca parameters docs](https://openfisca.org/doc/coding-the-legislation/legislation_parameters.html)
- USCIS policy alerts arrive as discrete dated documents (Policy Manual updates page), and law firms interpose attorney-authored analysis before advising clients (Fragomen/Duane Morris/Ogletree alerts) — no serious operator pipes raw government text to clients without professional review — [USCIS Policy Manual Updates](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/updates); [Fragomen](https://www.fragomen.com/insights/united-states-dhs-issues-final-rule-replacing-admission-for-duration-of-status-with-fixed-periods-of-stay-for-f-j-and-i-nonimmigrants.html)
- Moffatt v. Air Canada establishes that runtime AI output carries full company liability, which is the legal argument for gating high-stakes templates rather than trusting per-response generation — [ABA analysis](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/)

### Inferences
- Pre-publication human (attorney) approval required for: (1) every rule-pack diff (new/changed parameter values, formulas, transition logic) with its citation and source snapshot; (2) every broadcast "news affects your case" push template and its targeting query (which cohorts receive it); (3) activation/deactivation of a rule due to litigation (injunction handling); (4) any new question-type the narration layer is allowed to answer; (5) the canned out-of-scope/abstention copy.
- Runtime-only automated checks suffice for: per-user timeline renders from an approved pack (validators: dates ⊆ engine output, citations resolve, schema conformance); routine recomputation when a user edits their own data; reminder notifications generated from already-approved templates with engine-computed dates substituted.
- Escalation path: validator failure or `OUT_OF_SCOPE` never degrades to best-effort LLM text — it produces the abstention card and, above a frequency threshold, a founder/attorney review ticket (the signal a rule-pack gap exists).
- With one founder + part-time attorney, the approval unit must be small and asynchronous: git-PR-style diffs with citation links, reviewable in minutes; the OpenFisca `expected`-value pattern means the D/S-style rules (announced July, effective September) can be encoded, reviewed, and tested weeks before they go live.

### Gaps
- No published SLA/staffing data on how commercial immigration platforms (Envoy, Boundless) gate content behind attorney review; their supervising-attorney model is known publicly only at a marketing level, so the gating split above is a design inference from liability precedent, not a documented industry standard.

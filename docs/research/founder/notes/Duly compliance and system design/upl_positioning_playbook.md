# UPL Positioning and Disclaimer Playbook for Paid Consumer Immigration-Adjacent Software (Duly)

## KQ1: Disclaimer and consent structures used by incumbents (Boundless, LegalZoom, Rocket Lawyer, Nolo, Sprintax, TurboTax analogy, DoNotPay post-order)

### Takeaway
The industry-standard pattern is a three-layer disclaimer stack: (1) clickwrap terms with "not a law firm / no legal advice / no attorney-client relationship" language, (2) service-specific terms that wall off attorney services into a separate legal entity with independent attorneys, and (3) explicit user acknowledgments of limits (truthful inputs, no guaranteed outcome, limited scope). LegalZoom's terms are the canonical self-help template; Boundless adds all-caps in-flow disclaimers for non-attorney staff; Sprintax shows the same model works for the F-1/nonresident audience in tax.

### Cited Findings
- LegalZoom terms state: "LegalZoom is not a law firm and may not perform services performed by an attorney" — [LegalZoom Terms of Use](https://www.legalzoom.com/legal/general-terms/terms-of-use)
- LegalZoom terms deny doing anything advice-like, listing what it does NOT do: review answers for legal sufficiency, draw legal conclusions, provide opinions about rights/remedies/selection of forms/strategies, or "apply the law to the facts of your particular situation" — [LegalZoom Terms of Use](https://www.legalzoom.com/legal/general-terms/terms-of-use)
- LegalZoom frames content as "general information on commonly encountered legal issues," not guaranteed correct/complete/current, and states use "does not and will not create an attorney-client relationship" with LegalZoom; attorney network is framed as "attorney access services" introducing visitors to attorneys, with no attorney-client relationship with LegalZoom itself — [LegalZoom Terms of Use](https://www.legalzoom.com/legal/general-terms/terms-of-use)
- Boundless service-specific terms use in-flow, all-caps disclaimers for its non-attorney staff (B1/B2 service): "BOUNDLESS PERSONNEL THAT ASSIST YOU WITH COMPLETING YOUR APPLICATION ARE NOT ATTORNEYS AND DO NOT PROVIDE LEGAL ADVICE." — [Boundless Service-Specific Terms](https://www.boundless.com/service-specific-terms)
- Boundless "Ask My Attorney" terms: "Boundless is Not a Law Firm"; the attorney "is an independent practitioner and is not an employee of Boundless" who must exercise "independent legal judgment, without interference, influence, or control by Boundless"; representation is under a separate Limited Scope Representation Agreement and "ends when your call with the Attorney ends" — [Boundless Service-Specific Terms](https://www.boundless.com/service-specific-terms)
- Boundless requires affirmative user acknowledgments: information provided is truthful (attorney "will not make any independent investigation"), no promise of "a favorable result," and scope is limited to the consultation — [Boundless Service-Specific Terms](https://www.boundless.com/service-specific-terms)
- Sprintax (tax software for the same F-1/nonresident population) terms: "Sprintax is not providing legal or tax advice"; users acknowledge Sprintax will not sign returns or act as paid preparer; "The User is responsible for the accuracy" of inputs and "Sprintax will not audit or otherwise verify any information User provides" — [Sprintax Terms and Conditions](https://www.sprintax.com/terms-and-conditions.html)
- Rocket Lawyer operates the software side as a non-law-firm and the legal side through Rocket Legal Professional Services, Inc. (RLPS), a wholly-owned subsidiary approved as an Arizona ABS on Sept 24, 2024, employing licensed attorneys and paralegals; it had joined the Utah regulatory sandbox four years earlier — [Rocket Lawyer newsroom](https://www.rocketlawyer.com/newsroom/rlps-approved-for-abs-license)
- DoNotPay: FTC finalized its order Jan 16, 2025 (5-0 vote); DoNotPay must pay $193,000, is prohibited from claiming its service performs like a real lawyer without evidence/testing, and must notify 2021-2023 subscribers of the settlement; the case was triggered by "the world's first robot lawyer" marketing claims that were never tested against human-lawyer performance — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-finalizes-order-donotpay-prohibits-deceptive-ai-lawyer-claims-imposes-monetary-relief-requires)

### Inferences
- Disclaimers appear in three places across incumbents: signup clickwrap (LegalZoom/Sprintax pattern), service-specific terms accepted per product line (Boundless pattern), and in-flow all-caps notices at the moment a human or AI assists with an application (Boundless B1/B2 pattern). Duly should replicate all three layers plus per-AI-answer footers.
- Affirmative acknowledgments incumbents collect are about: (a) not-a-law-firm/no-advice, (b) truthfulness and completeness of user-supplied data, (c) no outcome guarantee, (d) limited scope of any attorney touch. These map directly to a Duly onboarding checkbox flow.
- The DoNotPay order shifts the marketing risk axis from pure UPL to FTC deception: the binding rule is "don't claim lawyer-equivalence or AI capabilities you haven't substantiated," which is enforceable federally regardless of state UPL law. Duly marketing must never say "replaces a lawyer/DSO," "AI immigration lawyer," or performance claims without testing evidence.

### Gaps
- Boundless's main terms-of-use page returned 404 on direct fetch; only the service-specific terms were retrievable, so the exact signup clickwrap acknowledgment wording for the general Boundless account could not be quoted.
- Nolo's terms page returned a 402 error; Nolo's exact current disclaimer wording is unverified (its historical positioning as self-help publisher is well known but should not be quoted without a source).
- TurboTax/Intuit terms were not fetched (budget); the tax-prep analogy is instead covered via Sprintax, which serves the identical F-1 audience.
- Per-answer footer wording used by any incumbent AI chat feature (e.g., LegalZoom's AI tools) was not directly observed; no source found quoting an in-product per-answer disclaimer verbatim.

## KQ2: Federal definition of practice/preparation in immigration (8 CFR 1001.1) and state self-help software carve-outs (Texas, North Carolina)

### Takeaway
Federally, "practice" turns on exercising professional judgment to give legal advice; "preparation" is safe only when it consists solely of filling blanks with applicant-provided information for a nominal fee and without holding oneself out as immigration-qualified. Texas gives software an explicit statutory safe harbor conditioned on one conspicuous sentence; North Carolina gives one conditioned on seven operational requirements plus State Bar registration.

### Cited Findings
- 8 CFR 1001.1(i) defines practice as "exercising professional judgment to provide legal advice or legal services related to any matter before EOIR" — [8 CFR 1001.1, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/8/1001.1)
- 8 CFR 1001.1(k) defines preparation as acts "consisting solely of filling in blank spaces on printed forms" with information provided by the applicant; it excludes any exercise of professional judgment, requires that any non-practitioner fee "must be nominal," and the preparer may not hold themselves out "as qualified in legal matters or in immigration and naturalization procedure" — [8 CFR 1001.1, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/8/1001.1)
- Texas Gov't Code 81.101(c): the "practice of law" does not include computer software and similar products, provided the "products clearly and conspicuously state that the products are not a substitute for the advice of an attorney" — [FindLaw, Tex. Gov't Code 81.101](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/government-code/gov-t-sect-81-101/)
- The Texas carve-out originated in 1999 legislation (H.B. 1507, 76th Legislature) enacted amid litigation over Quicken Family Lawyer self-help software — [Texas H.B. 1507 Bill Analysis](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/76R/analysis/html/HB01507F.htm)
- N.C. Gen. Stat. 84-2.2 exempts self-help legal software only if all conditions are met: consumer can see the blank template or final completed document before purchase; an NC-licensed attorney reviewed each template (reviewer on file); provider communicates "the forms or templates are not a substitute for the advice or services of an attorney"; provider discloses legal name and address; provider does NOT disclaim warranties or limit damages; provider does not force out-of-state venue; UPL complaints must be referred to the NC State Bar with a conspicuously displayed consumer satisfaction process; plus annual registration with the NC State Bar (initial fee ≤$100, renewal ≤$50) — [N.C.G.S. 84-2.2](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_84/GS_84-2.2.html)

### Inferences
- 8 CFR 1001.1's advice/preparation line technically governs practice before EOIR/DHS; the operative design rule it encodes — form-filling with user-supplied data is clerical, choosing what to file or advising on eligibility is practice — is the analytical frame regulators and CLINIC-style trainers apply to immigration helpers generally. Software that only computes and displays the user's own dates and stores their own documents sits on the clerical side; software that selects immigration benefits, forms, or answers for a specific user's situation drifts toward "professional judgment."
- The Texas safe harbor is the cheapest compliance win in the country: one conspicuous sentence ("not a substitute for the advice of an attorney") purchases explicit statutory cover, so that sentence should be Duly's baseline disclaimer everywhere, verbatim.
- The NC statute is effectively a national compliance checklist because it was drafted with LegalZoom to describe what a legitimate self-help product looks like: show the output before purchase, have a licensed reviewer behind templates/content, don't fully disclaim liability, don't force hostile venue, publish a complaint process. Any of these that Duly adopts nationally reduces UPL exposure in every state.
- Note the NC tension for standard SaaS terms: NC's safe harbor forbids disclaiming warranties/limiting damages, while typical SaaS terms do exactly that. Products either carve NC users out of the limitation-of-liability clause or forgo the NC statutory harbor and rely on general positioning.

### Gaps
- No 2024-2026 enforcement action applying 8 CFR 1001.1 or state notario-fraud statutes specifically to consumer software was found within budget; CLINIC's UPIL training materials were not fetched, so the practitioner-consensus reading of "nominal assistance" for software could not be quoted from a primary CLINIC source.

## KQ3: Precedents shaping the line (Janson, LegalZoom v. NC Bar, FTC/DoNotPay, ABA Op. 512, state bar AI opinions 2024-2026)

### Takeaway
Janson establishes that interactive document assembly can be UPL because legal judgment is embedded in the branching logic ("the software is the advice"); the NC settlement shows the negotiated exit (attorney-reviewed templates + disclaimers + document preview); the FTC DoNotPay order adds a federal deception overlay on AI-lawyer marketing; ABA Op. 512 and the 2024-2025 state opinions (FL 24-1, TX 705, DC 388) govern lawyers using AI and matter to Duly mainly when attorneys are in the loop.

### Cited Findings
- Janson v. LegalZoom.com, Inc., 802 F. Supp. 2d 1053 (W.D. Mo. 2011): court denied LegalZoom summary judgment on UPL, distinguishing permissible sale of blank forms from LegalZoom's active preparation of customized documents; the court reasoned "A computer sitting at a desk in California cannot prepare a legal document" without a human programming legal principles into it — the tech medium was immaterial — [Eric Goldman, Technology & Marketing Law Blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2011/08/missouri_federa_1.htm)
- The same analysis flagged that LegalZoom's own marketing ("we'll take care of it," "real helpful people") undercut the self-help defense by positioning LegalZoom, not the customer, as the preparer — [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2011/08/missouri_federa_1.htm)
- LegalZoom v. NC State Bar 2015 settlement: LegalZoom agreed to vet its documents with North Carolina lawyers and to inform customers the templates aren't a substitute for in-person attorney advice; both sides agreed to support legislation clarifying UPL and permitting interactive legal-help websites operating on those terms — [ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/legalzoom_resolves_10.5m_antitrust_suit_against_north_carolina_state_bar) (the resulting statute is N.C.G.S. 84-2.2, cited in KQ2)
- FTC v. DoNotPay final order (Jan 16, 2025): $193,000 monetary relief; prohibition on unsubstantiated claims that the service performs like a real lawyer; mandatory notice to 2021-2023 subscribers; basis was that DoNotPay "never tested" whether its AI operated at a lawyer's level and hired no attorneys to verify output accuracy — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-finalizes-order-donotpay-prohibits-deceptive-ai-lawyer-claims-imposes-monetary-relief-requires)
- Florida Bar Ethics Opinion 24-1 (2024): lawyers may use generative AI but "must protect the confidentiality of client information, provide accurate and competent services," avoid improper billing, and comply with advertising rules — [Florida Bar Opinion 24-1](https://www.floridabar.org/etopinions/opinion-24-1/)
- Texas issued Opinion 705 (Texas Center for Legal Ethics, Feb 2025) addressing lawyers' use of generative AI — [Opinion 705, Texas Center for Legal Ethics](https://www.legalethicstexas.com/resources/opinions/opinion-705/)
- A maintained state-by-state tracker of bar AI guidance (covering ABA Op. 512, DC Bar Op. 388, FL 24-1, TX 705, and others) is at [Steno, Legal AI Rules by State](https://brief.steno.com/legal-ai-rules-by-state)

### Inferences
- Janson's operating rule for product design: the more the software's branching logic selects legal outcomes for a specific user (which form, which answer, which category), the more it looks like the programmer practicing law at scale. Keeping the user as the visible decision-maker at every branch ("you choose; here is what the rule says") is the design translation.
- The FTC order effectively bans three phrases from AI-legal marketing without substantiation: lawyer replacement claims, lawyer-equivalence performance claims, and untested accuracy claims. It also implies an affirmative practice: document internal accuracy testing and attorney review of AI outputs so capability claims are substantiated.
- ABA Op. 512's full text was not fetched, but its role for Duly is downstream: any partner attorneys reviewing Duly content or serving Duly users must satisfy competence/confidentiality/supervision duties for GenAI, so Duly's attorney-facing tooling should expose sources and allow verification (which also matches Duly's citations-first design).

### Gaps
- ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 29, 2024) full text was not fetched directly; findings on it here are limited to its existence and general subject (lawyer duties using GenAI: competence, confidentiality, communication, fees, supervision) as reflected in secondary trackers ([Steno tracker](https://brief.steno.com/legal-ai-rules-by-state)); exact quotes should be pulled from the ABA PDF before publication use.
- California's guidance (State Bar Practical Guidance for Generative AI, Nov 2023) and DC Bar Op. 388 specifics were not fetched; only tracker-level confirmation of their existence.
- No state bar opinion specifically addressing consumer-facing AI legal chatbots (as opposed to lawyer use of AI) was found in 2024-2026 searches; this appears to be a genuine regulatory gap, currently policed via FTC deception authority (DoNotPay) rather than bar ethics opinions.

## KQ4: Derived product-design rules for Duly (what an AI answer may say; deadline math; DSO drafts; evidence packs)

### Takeaway
Combining 8 CFR 1001.1(k), Janson, the Texas/NC safe harbors, and incumbent practice yields concrete per-feature rules: state general rules with citations, show computations from the user's own data, keep the user as chooser at every branch, never select forms/benefits for a specific user, and route individualized "what should I do" moments to a DSO or attorney.

### Cited Findings
- The legal-advice line is "exercising professional judgment" applied to a particular person's facts (8 CFR 1001.1(i)); filling blanks "with information provided by the applicant" is not practice (8 CFR 1001.1(k)) — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/8/1001.1)
- LegalZoom's terms enumerate the exact activities a self-help product must disclaim doing: reviewing answers for legal sufficiency, drawing legal conclusions, recommending selection of forms, applying law to the user's particular situation — [LegalZoom Terms of Use](https://www.legalzoom.com/legal/general-terms/terms-of-use)
- Janson: embedding legal decision-making in software branching, applied to customer inputs, was treated as potential UPL, unlike selling blank forms — [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2011/08/missouri_federa_1.htm)
- Sprintax demonstrates the accepted pattern for the F-1 audience: software performs rule-based computations (residency determination, treaty eligibility) from user inputs while disclaiming legal/tax advice and putting accuracy responsibility on the user — [Sprintax Terms](https://www.sprintax.com/terms-and-conditions.html)
- NC's safe harbor conditions a template product on letting the consumer see the completed document before purchase and on professional review of every template — [N.C.G.S. 84-2.2](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_84/GS_84-2.2.html)

### Inferences (the per-feature playbook; these are derived rules, not quoted law)
- Safe AI-answer template: (1) state the general rule with a citation to the regulation/USCIS page; (2) describe what people in a generic situation category typically consider; (3) end with a routing line to the user's DSO or a licensed attorney. Forbidden shapes: "you should file Form X," "you qualify for Y," "answer question 7 with Z" — those apply law to the user's particular facts (the LegalZoom-disclaimed list and 8 CFR 1001.1(i) territory).
- Deadline computation from the user's own dates (e.g., 60-day grace period end, 90-day unemployment clock, OPT application windows) is arithmetic on user-supplied data against published rules, analogous to Sprintax's residency-day counting; safe as self-help IF the output is framed as "based on the dates you entered, the published rule's window is..." with the source rule cited, and never as "you must/should file by" advice. Label computed dates as estimates dependent on the user's inputs being correct (mirror Sprintax's user-responsibility clause).
- Prefilled DSO email drafts: low risk when they are communication templates the user edits and sends (comparable to blank-form provision), because the DSO — a designated school official with actual authority — remains the decision-maker; keep drafts factual ("I would like to ask about...") rather than asserting legal conclusions on the user's behalf.
- Evidence-pack / document-vault assembly: storing, organizing, and checklisting the user's own documents against a published USCIS/SEVP checklist is clerical organization, not judgment — but "your evidence is sufficient/insufficient" is a legal-sufficiency review (explicitly on LegalZoom's disclaimed list); ship completeness-against-published-checklist indicators ("the USCIS page lists these items; you have uploaded 4 of 6"), never sufficiency judgments.
- Do NOT auto-select or auto-prepare government forms for users at launch: that is exactly the Janson fact pattern and the 8 CFR 1001.1(k) trap (Duly is also not a nominal-fee preparer since it charges subscription). If form help is ever added, either keep it to blank official forms + instructions, or route through a licensed structure (KQ5).
- Operationalizing "we suggest, you decide, consult an attorney": every recommendation-shaped surface (checklist item, deadline card, AI answer) should carry the Texas-safe-harbor sentence or equivalent, and the user's affirmative action (checking off, confirming dates, sending the draft) should be the recorded decision event — the audit trail shows the user deciding at each branch.
- Marketing constraint from DoNotPay: never claim Duly replaces attorneys or DSOs, and keep internal documentation of accuracy testing/attorney content review before making any reliability claim.

### Gaps
- No authority directly blesses or condemns deadline calculators or email-draft generators; the safety analysis above is inference from the form/advice line, not a cited holding. No case found (2024-2026) testing an AI immigration chatbot for UPL on the merits.

## KQ5: When/how products add attorneys (LegalZoom network, Boundless independent attorneys, Rocket Lawyer ABS, AZ/UT sandboxes)

### Takeaway
Incumbents stay pure self-help until a feature requires applying law to an individual's facts (form selection/preparation with judgment, case strategy, representation); then they bolt on attorneys via one of two structures: (a) independent-attorney referral/limited-scope model with the software company outside the attorney-client relationship (LegalZoom network, Boundless "Ask My Attorney"), or (b) owning the law practice outright through an Arizona ABS or Utah sandbox entity (Rocket Lawyer RLPS; Boundless runs "Boundless Legal Services LLC" out of Scottsdale, AZ).

### Cited Findings
- LegalZoom describes its attorney offering as "attorney access services" that "introduce our visitors to attorneys" via legal plans, directories, and limited scope agreements, with no attorney-client relationship formed with LegalZoom — [LegalZoom Terms of Use](https://www.legalzoom.com/legal/general-terms/terms-of-use)
- Boundless's attorney model: independent practitioner attorneys, not Boundless employees, under separate Limited Scope Representation Agreements; Boundless pays the attorney a fixed portion of the user's fee (attorney's property, not trust funds) and may pay additional compensation; independence from Boundless "interference, influence, or control" is contractually required — [Boundless Service-Specific Terms](https://www.boundless.com/service-specific-terms)
- Rocket Lawyer's subsidiary RLPS was approved as an Arizona ABS on Sept 24, 2024 — Arizona being "the nation's first and only permanent" regime allowing nonlawyer ownership of law firms — after Rocket Lawyer joined the Utah regulatory sandbox ~2020 to serve underserved communities — [Rocket Lawyer newsroom](https://www.rocketlawyer.com/newsroom/rlps-approved-for-abs-license)
- A "Boundless Legal Services LLC" entity is registered in Scottsdale, Arizona — [Bizapedia listing](https://www.bizapedia.com/az/boundless-legal-services-llc.html); Arizona's ABS committee meeting minutes (Jan 2026) show the ABS licensing pipeline remains active — [AZ Courts ABS minutes](https://www.azcourts.gov/Portals/0/ABS%20Regular%20Mins%20January%2013,%202026%20Final%20Draft.pdf)
- Analysis of Arizona's ABS program and out-of-state friction (neighboring-state resistance to ABS entities serving clients elsewhere) is discussed in [Arizona State Law Journal, Jan 2026](https://arizonastatelawjournal.org/2026/01/27/arizonas-alternative-business-structures-innovation-meets-neighboring-resistance/) and [IAALS](https://iaals.du.edu/news/alternative-business-structures-us-what-we-know-and-what-we-still-need-learn)

### Inferences
- Trigger points for adding attorneys, per incumbent behavior: individualized case assessment ("is my situation OK?"), form preparation involving judgment, and anything marketed as review/representation. Duly's stated scope (checklists, deadline suggestions, cited scenario answers, vault) does not yet cross these; a future "review my OPT application" or "file for me" feature would.
- Two viable structures when that day comes: the Boundless independent-attorney model (fast, works in all states, requires strict independence + limited-scope agreements + fee structured as flat portion, and fee-splitting rules navigated by making the payment the attorney's earned property) or the AZ ABS route (own the firm; note immigration practice before USCIS is federal, which softens the state-by-state ABS friction problem, since any US-licensed attorney can practice federal immigration law nationwide).
- One special immigration advantage not available to LegalZoom's state-law products: immigration practice authorization is federal (attorney licensed in any state can represent before DHS/EOIR), so a single ABS or partner firm can serve F-1 users in all 50 states — a reason the Boundless independent-attorney model scales cheaply for immigration specifically. (Inference from the federal nature of 8 CFR practice rules; no single citation.)

### Gaps
- Could not confirm from a primary source (AZ Courts license list or Boundless announcement) that Boundless Legal Services LLC holds an ABS license, only its Scottsdale AZ registration; verify against the Arizona Supreme Court ABS directory before relying on it as precedent.
- Marble and Hello Divorce referral-structure details were not researched within budget.

## KQ6: Clickwrap enforceability essentials (Meyer v. Uber line) for the "not legal advice, I understand" acknowledgment

### Takeaway
Under Meyer v. Uber (2d Cir. 2017), an online agreement binds when the user had reasonably conspicuous notice of the terms and unambiguously manifested assent; the safest implementation for Duly's UPL acknowledgment is true clickwrap (unticked checkbox + hyperlinked terms adjacent to the action button), repeated as a standalone acknowledgment screen for the not-legal-advice statement itself.

### Cited Findings
- Meyer v. Uber Technologies, Inc., 868 F.3d 66 (2d Cir. 2017) held Uber's registration screen enforceable where the terms hyperlink appeared directly below the registration button with language warning that registering constitutes agreement; the court's test: reasonably conspicuous notice plus unambiguous manifestation of assent — [Justia, Meyer v. Uber, No. 16-2750](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/16-2750/16-2750-2017-08-17.html); analysis at [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2017/08/meyervuber.htm) and [Proskauer New Media & Technology Law Blog](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2017/08/30/second-circuit-upholds-ubers-mobile-contracting-process-establishing-template-for-mobile-online-contracting/)
- The Ninth Circuit line of cases similarly requires conspicuous notice and affirmative assent for online arbitration/terms agreements to stick — [WSHB analysis of Ninth Circuit guidance](https://www.wshblaw.com/experience-ninth-circuit-provides-primer-on-creating-online-arbitration-agreements-that-stick)

### Inferences
- Design spec for Duly's acknowledgment flow (derived from Meyer factors + incumbent practice): (1) signup screen: unticked checkbox, "I agree to the Terms of Service and understand Duly is not a law firm and does not provide legal advice," with underlined contrasting hyperlink to terms, uncluttered screen; (2) a second dedicated interstitial before first AI answer restating the Texas-safe-harbor sentence and requiring a tap ("I understand"); (3) log timestamp, screen version, and text version of each acknowledgment for evidentiary use; (4) re-prompt on material terms changes. Browsewrap (terms only in footer) should be assumed unenforceable.
- Repeating short-form disclaimers per AI answer is not needed for contract enforceability (the clickwrap handles that) but serves the Texas "clearly and conspicuously" statutory condition and rebuts any later claim that a specific answer was reasonably understood as individualized legal advice.

### Gaps
- No case found testing enforceability of a "not legal advice" acknowledgment specifically (as opposed to arbitration clauses); courts evaluate the assent mechanics identically, but a disclaimer's substantive effect against a UPL claim (brought by a regulator, not the user) is untested — a state bar is not bound by the user's contract, which is why product-design compliance (KQ4) matters more than the disclaimer alone.
